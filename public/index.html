The four-card trick

"Road safety" policy leans on a handful of claims that sound obvious and are almost never shown in full. Here is what each one hides — each unpacked with sources on its own page.

1. "Speed kills — so any enforcement saves lives."

Official figures say speed is "a factor" in roughly one in four to one in three fatal crashes — but that number bundles together illegal high-range speeding, driving too fast for conditions (often under the limit), and cases where speed merely worsened a crash caused by something else. There is no published Australian figure for "crashes caused by exceeding the limit on an otherwise safe road" — the exact thing low-level camera fines target. Unpack the speed numbers →

2. "The limit is the safe speed."

Only Victoria publishes its enforcement tolerance — 2 km/h on fixed cameras (tightened from 3 in 2019). Everywhere else it's confidential and, legally, any amount over the line is an offence. The century-old 85th-percentile engineering rule that used to anchor limits to how people actually drive is quietly being retired. See how limits are set →

3. "Cameras go where the crashes are."

Victoria's Auditor-General found in 2026 that 15 of 33 sampled AI camera sites had no crash record at all, and police "cannot show" they checked crash history. When NSW stripped the warning signs off mobile speed cameras in late 2020, low-range fines jumped from ~3,200 a month to ~27,900 — then fell ~90% the moment the signs came back in 2023. If the goal is slowing people at a hazard, why hide the camera? Follow the money →

4. "If you've done nothing wrong, you've nothing to fear."

The same networks that issue fines photograph the front cabin of every car that passes and read every plate — innocent or not. Average-speed cameras built "for trucks" now time ordinary cars. ANPR gathered "for road safety" has already been repurposed to enforce COVID lockdowns. The fine is the excuse; the surveillance is the product. See the surveillance grid →

The independent Australian Automobile Association's benchmarking puts the toll roughly 20% above where it would need to be to stay on track, and rising year-on-year for over two years straight. Their verdict: the plan "looks more like a wish-list than a credible strategy."
