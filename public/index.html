An exposé

They said it was about safety.

Every camera, every lowered limit, every new fine is sold with the same three words: speed kills, safety first. But when you read the government's own audits, the crash data and the budget papers, a different machine comes into focus — one built to raise revenue and to watch where everyone drives.

The four-card trick

"Road safety" policy leans on a handful of claims that sound obvious and are almost never shown in full. Here is what each one hides.

1. "Speed kills — so any speed enforcement saves lives."

Speed is a factor in a minority of serious crashes; the leading contributors in the data are usually a mix of inattention, fatigue, alcohol and drugs, and failure to give way. A camera clipping a driver for 3 km/h over on a clear, dry, empty road is not touching any of those. "Speed kills" quietly slides from excessive speed in the wrong conditions to any speed over an arbitrary line — because the second version is the one you can automate and bill.

2. "The limit is the safe speed."

Traffic engineers have a century-old tool for setting limits: the 85th percentile — the speed at or below which 85% of drivers naturally travel on a road. Set the limit there and enforcement targets genuine outliers. Set it well below, and you criminalise the safest, most typical driver on the road — and you manufacture a permanent, predictable stream of "offenders." Many camera-heavy limits sit conspicuously below the 85th percentile.

3. "Cameras go where the crashes are."

If enforcement were about crashes, camera locations would track blackspots. Audits keep finding the opposite: cameras clustered on gentle downhill straights, at the bottom of hills, just past a limit drop — locations optimised for detections, not for danger. In NSW, warning signs were stripped from mobile speed cameras in 2020 and fine volumes exploded; signage was only partially restored after public backlash. If the goal is to slow people at a hazard, why hide the camera?

4. "If you've done nothing wrong, you've nothing to fear."

The same networks that issue fines photograph and log every plate that passes, innocent or not. Those reads build a map of where you go and when. The fine is the excuse; the surveillance is the product. "Nothing to hide" has never been the test for whether the state gets to keep a movement history on millions of people who were never accused of anything.

Follow the money

The tell is in the budget. When a program is really about safety, success means the numbers fall and the revenue dries up. When it's about revenue, the fines are forecast to grow — and treasuries quietly rely on them.

Governments publish this themselves. Auditors-general in multiple states have reported that camera programs are run without clear evidence they were sited for safety, that revenue was a central consideration, and that removing warnings drove fine numbers up. The surveillance you didn't vote for

A fine is a one-off. A movement record is forever. The road-safety brand is the wrapper on a mass automatic-number-plate-recognition (ANPR) network that grew with almost no debate.

Every plate, every pass

Fixed, point-to-point and mobile cameras read plates continuously — not just the "speeders." Non-offenders are photographed too. The reads don't vanish; they're stored and cross-matched.

Point-to-point creep

"Average speed" cameras were introduced for heavy trucks on safety grounds — then quietly extended to ordinary cars. The infrastructure to time everyone between two points was already there.

Phone-detection AI

Cameras that photograph inside your car to catch phone use point high-resolution lenses at drivers and passengers by default. The tech that spots a phone can see everything else in the cabin.

Function creep

Data gathered "for road safety" gets requested for other purposes later. Once the network exists and the data is retained, every future government inherits a ready-made tracking grid.

What actually saves lives

None of this means roads should be a free-for-all. It means we should demand the interventions the evidence actually supports — the boring, un-billable ones.

  • Better road design and engineering at real blackspots
  • Median barriers and run-off protection where head-on and single-vehicle crashes cluster
  • Limits set honestly to the 85th percentile, then enforced against genuine outliers
  • Visible, warned enforcement that changes behaviour before the danger — not hidden traps that bank a fine after
  • Serious action on the top killers: fatigue, impairment, and distraction
  • Every enforcement dollar ring-fenced for road safety, publicly, with the data released

Sources & how to check us

Don't take our word for it — that's the whole point. These are the categories of public record that back the claims above. Search them for your own state.

  • Auditor-General / performance audit reports on speed and red-light camera programs (e.g. NSW Audit Office; Victorian Auditor-General's Office). Look for findings on siting, warning signage and revenue.
  • State budget papers — search "fines and forfeitures" and camera revenue line items and forward estimates.
  • BITRE (Bureau of Infrastructure and Transport Research Economics) crash and fatality statistics — for actual crash causation, not campaign slogans.
  • Traffic-engineering literature on the 85th percentile and speed-limit setting (e.g. Austroads guidance; US FHWA methodology).
  • Freedom-of-Information requests on ANPR data retention, access logs, and inter-agency sharing.
  • News reporting on the removal and partial reinstatement of NSW mobile speed camera warning signs (2020–2023) and fine-volume changes.
